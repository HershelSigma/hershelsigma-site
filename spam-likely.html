Why your calls show Spam Likely, and how outbound floors fix it

By Nathan Yonash, Founder, Hershel Sigma AI · Updated October 4, 2026

Calls show Spam Likely, Scam Likely or Potential Spam when a carrier's call-analytics partner scores the number as unwanted, based largely on calling behavior and complaints. Labels are per number and per carrier. Floors handle them by registering numbers, asking each engine for review, retiring numbers that stay flagged and changing the calling patterns behind the flag.

On the solar floors I run in Texas and Florida, a flagged number usually shows up first as a list that seems to have gone cold, with answer rates sliding while nobody changed a thing.

Who decides a number is Spam Likely

Your carrier shows the label, but a call-analytics company usually decides it. USTelecom's call labeling contact list pairs each of the big three US wireless carriers with its analytics partner: AT&T with Hiya, T-Mobile with First Orion, and Verizon with TNS (Transaction Network Services).

The wording differs too. T-Mobile shows Scam Likely, Verizon shows Potential Spam, and AT&T labels calls Spam Risk or Fraud Risk; its ActiveArmor terms say spam-risk calls are sorted using customer complaints and feedback. Since every engine scores on its own data, a number can be labeled on one network and clean on another. Hiya says results vary by operator, region and time.

What gets a number flagged

Mostly how the number behaves and how people react to it. Hiya's support site lists the common reasons: many people reporting the number, a calling pattern that matches spam (high volume, short calls), a previous owner who got it flagged, or trouble with its carrier or number range. TNS points to high call volumes, low engagement and user complaints.

That's an awkward list for an outbound floor, because a dialer does much of it by design. It calls strangers at volume, and on a cold list most calls go unanswered or end fast. Pacing makes it worse, since a dialer that dials too hard sends answered calls to dead air and people who hear silence hang up fast. Hiya's advice to businesses includes making sure auto-dialers are set up properly, which is a big part of what an AI dialer manages.

Authentication matters too. Under STIR/SHAKEN the originating carrier signs each call with an attestation level, and the FCC describes three. A (full) means it can confirm the caller and the caller's right to the number, B (partial) means it knows the caller but not the number, and C (gateway) means it's only the entry point for a call from elsewhere. Receiving carriers can use it to protect customers from unwanted and spoofed calls, so ask your carrier what attestation yours get.

How to check whether your numbers are flagged

Call phones on each major network from each number and look at the screen. Hiya's guide to checking for spam labels starts there, then adds searching your numbers in spam-blocking apps and watching call reports for falling answer rates and shorter calls. One test call shows one network at one moment, so repeat it.

Your dialer's data warns you sooner if you split answer rate and call length by caller ID, since a campaign average can hide one dying number. Hiya's free Number Registration also shows a number's spam status, and First Orion says its free registration tells you when you're tagged on T-Mobile and why.

How to get a label reviewed or removed

Register first, then ask each engine for a review, and don't expect promises. The Free Caller Registry, run by First Orion, Hiya and TNS, sends one free form to all three. It takes up to 20 US numbers per submission and is meant for the business that actually uses them, not a vendor registering for a client. The registry says plainly that registering doesn't guarantee redress, and that submitting a number twice adds nothing.

If the label stays, use each carrier's review path. AT&T's review address forwards to a Hiya support form. Verizon's feedback form warns that a submission may not change how future calls are labeled, and Hiya says registration lowers the risk of a label without preventing one, since carriers still weigh how people respond to your calls.

How to keep numbers healthy over time

Check reputation every day. TNS says it keeps moving with call behavior and user feedback, and that even trusted numbers can get flagged.

Start with the calls. Clean lists, sensible hours, honoring requests not to be called again and following the calling rules that apply to your floor all cut the blocks and complaints that feed the engines. Keep the volume on each number reasonable, register every number before it goes live, and check new numbers first, since a previous owner's history can come with them.

When a number stays flagged after registration and a review request, retire it. Hammering a labeled number burns agent hours on calls few people answer. Replace it with a registered, checked number and fix what caused the flag, or the new one ends up in the same place. Frequently asked questions

Why do my calls show Spam Likely when my business is legitimate?

The engines score numbers mostly on behavior, not ownership. High volume, short calls, blocks and spam reports can get any number labeled, and a label can carry over from a previous owner. AT&T's ActiveArmor terms admit the service may misidentify some numbers as spam risk.

How do I remove Spam Likely from my number?

Register it through the Free Caller Registry, then request a review on each carrier's path: AT&T's review form, T-Mobile's call reporting site and Verizon's voice spam feedback form. None of them promise removal, so fix the calling pattern behind the flag too, and retire the number if the label stays.

Is Scam Likely different from Spam Likely?

It's the same kind of warning in different words. T-Mobile shows Scam Likely, Verizon shows Potential Spam, and AT&T shows Spam Risk or Fraud Risk. Spam Likely is the catch-all name most people use.

Does STIR/SHAKEN keep my calls from being labeled spam?

Not on its own. It tells the receiving carrier how well the originating carrier knows the caller and its right to the number, with A as the strongest attestation. That's about identity, not whether people want the call, so blocks and reports still count.
